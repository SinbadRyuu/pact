Get Started

The 7 foundations

Go through these in order. Each one builds on the last.

1Your outgoings vs your income▸

You can't invest money you don't have. Step one is knowing exactly what comes in and what goes out every month.

  • Write down your take-home pay (after tax) and any other income.
  • List every outgoing: rent/mortgage, bills, food, transport, debt, subscriptions, going out.
  • Go through 2–3 months of bank statements — most people underestimate their spending.
  • Whatever is left over is the money that can build your future.
2Saving & habits▸

Wealth is built by habits, not one big win.

  • Pay yourself first: set up an automatic transfer to savings on payday, before you spend anything.
  • Emergency fund: aim for 3–6 months of essential outgoings in an easy-access savings account before taking investment risk.
  • Kill expensive debt: credit cards and loans charging 20%+ cost more than most investments are likely to earn — clear them first.
  • Cut leaks: unused subscriptions and impulse buys add up fast.
  • Raise your savings rate every time your income goes up, instead of raising your lifestyle.
3Safer starter investments▸

When your emergency fund is in place, start simple and lower-risk:

  • High-interest savings & fixed-term accounts — low risk, but returns may not beat inflation over time.
  • Government bonds — lending to the government for a fixed return.
  • Global index funds / ETFs — one fund holding hundreds or thousands of companies. Still goes up and down, but spreads your risk widely and usually has low fees.
  • Use tax-efficient accounts where available (for example ISAs and pensions in the UK) — see the tab.

"Safer" doesn't mean risk-free. Any investment can fall in value.

4How to diversify▸

Diversifying means not putting all your eggs in one basket, so one bad investment can't wipe you out.

  • Across asset types: cash, stocks, bonds, property, precious metals, and (a small slice of) higher-risk assets.
  • Across countries and sectors: don't only own one country or one industry.
  • Across time: invest regularly (e.g. monthly) instead of all at once — known as pound-cost averaging.
  • Size by risk: the riskier the asset, the smaller the share of your money it should get.
5How to compound▸

Compounding is when your returns start earning returns of their own. Over long periods it does most of the heavy lifting.

  • Start early: time in the market is the biggest lever you have.
  • Be consistent: regular contributions matter more than perfect timing.
  • Reinvest: let dividends and interest roll back in rather than spending them.
  • Keep costs low: fees compound too — against you.
6Earn money from multiple incomes▸

One income is a single point of failure. More income streams = more to invest and more security.

  • Active income: your job, overtime, freelancing, a side business.
  • Skill income: high-income skills such as trading, sales, coding or a trade — see the tab.
  • Investment income: dividends, interest, rent from property.
  • Build them one at a time — get one stable before starting the next.
7Reinvest profits to gain complete freedom▸

Financial freedom is when your investments can pay for your lifestyle without you having to work.

  • Work out your "freedom number": your yearly spending × 25 is a commonly used rule of thumb (based on withdrawing around 4% a year — a guideline, not a guarantee).
  • Reinvest profits and income instead of spending them until you get there.
  • Rebalance once or twice a year so your risk stays where you want it.
  • Track progress with the calculator — put your freedom number in as your target.

Trading

Trading means buying and selling over shorter periods (minutes to weeks) to profit from price moves, rather than holding for years. Treated as a skill — learned, practised and measured — it can become an extra income stream. Most beginners lose money, so the aim early on is to survive and learn.

The foundations

  • Risk management first: many traders risk only 1–2% of their account on any single trade.
  • Always use a stop-loss — decide where you're wrong before you enter.
  • Have a written plan: what you trade, when you enter, when you exit.
  • Keep a journal of every trade and review it weekly.
  • Practise on a demo account before using real money.

Watch out for

  • Leverage (CFDs, spread bets, futures) multiplies losses as well as gains — you can lose more than you expect very quickly.
  • Over-trading and revenge trading after a loss.
  • "Guaranteed" signal groups and get-rich-quick promises.
  • Trading money you need for bills or your emergency fund.

Physical assetsMedium risk

Gold, Silver & Copper (physical)

Physical metals are real things you can hold. Gold in particular has been used as a store of value for thousands of years, and people often buy it as a hedge against inflation, currency weakness and market stress.

Gold

The classic "safe haven". Tends to hold value over long periods but can go years without growing. Coins and bars are the most common ways to own it physically.

Silver

Both a precious and an industrial metal (used in solar panels and electronics). Cheaper per ounce than gold and usually more volatile.

Copper

An industrial metal tied to construction, electrification and EVs. Physical copper is bulky to store, so its price is closely linked to the health of the economy.

Things to know before buying physical metal

  • Premiums: you pay above the "spot" price when you buy and usually get less than spot when you sell.
  • Storage & insurance: keep it secure — a home safe or a professional vault.
  • Buy from reputable dealers and check what you receive — fakes exist.
  • No income: metal doesn't pay dividends or interest; your return is only the price change.
  • Tax: rules differ by coin, bar and country — check the tab.

Shares & funds

Stocks

Owning a stock means owning a small piece of a company. Over long periods, stock markets have historically been one of the strongest-growing asset classes — but with big ups and downs along the way.

Safer approach

Steady & diversified

  • Global or S&P 500 index funds/ETFs — thousands or hundreds of companies in one fund, with low fees.
  • Large, established "blue-chip" companies with long track records.
  • Dividend payers — reinvest the dividends to compound.
  • Invest monthly and hold for the long term (5+ years).
Higher risk

Bigger potential, bigger swings

  • Individual growth stocks — small and mid-sized companies, tech and emerging sectors.
  • Penny stocks — very cheap shares that can swing (or collapse) wildly.
  • Concentrated positions — a few companies instead of hundreds.
  • Keep this to a small part of your portfolio and research each company properly.

Digital assetsHigh risk

Crypto

Cryptocurrencies are digital assets that run on blockchains. Even the largest ones can fall 50% or more, and many smaller coins have gone to zero. Treat it as a high-risk part of your portfolio, sized so you could handle losing it.

"Safer" end

Established coins

  • Bitcoin and Ethereum — the largest, longest-running and most widely held.
  • Buy regularly in small amounts rather than all at once.
  • Use large, reputable exchanges registered with your country's regulator.
  • Consider moving long-term holdings to a wallet you control (hardware wallet) — and never share your seed phrase.
Very high risk

Small coins & speculation

  • Small-cap "altcoins", meme coins and new token launches.
  • Leveraged crypto trading.
  • Watch out for rug pulls, pump-and-dumps, fake giveaways and anyone asking for your wallet keys.
  • If it promises guaranteed returns, it's a red flag.

Alternative assetsHigh risk

Collectibles — Pokémon tips & tricks

Trading cards, sealed products and other collectibles can rise a lot in value when supply is limited and demand grows. They can also fall when hype fades, and they're slower to sell than stocks.

Tips & tricks

  • Sealed product (booster boxes, elite trainer boxes) is often held long term — once it's opened, supply only goes down.
  • Graded cards: a professional grade (e.g. PSA, CGC, Beckett) can add a lot of value to top-condition cards.
  • Condition is everything — sleeves, top-loaders, and dry, dark storage.
  • Check sold prices, not asking prices, before you buy or sell.
  • Iconic characters and first editions have historically held demand best.

Watch out for

  • Fakes and resealed packs — buy from trusted sellers.
  • Fees: grading, postage, insurance and selling-platform fees all eat into profit.
  • Hype cycles — prices can drop fast after a peak.
  • Liquidity — it can take time to find a buyer at your price.

Real estateMedium risk

Property & the best kinds

Property can earn you two ways: rental income and the property rising in value. It usually needs more money to start, ties your money up for longer, and comes with ongoing costs and responsibilities.

Common types

  • Buy-to-let: a single house or flat rented to tenants.
  • HMOs (houses in multiple occupation): rooms let individually — usually higher income, more management and licensing rules.
  • Holiday / short-term lets: higher nightly rates, more work and seasonality.
  • Commercial: shops, offices, units — longer leases, different risks.
  • REITs / property funds: invest in property through the stock market with small amounts and no landlord duties.

What to check

  • Rental yield: yearly rent ÷ purchase price × 100.
  • All the costs: mortgage, deposit, stamp duty, legal fees, repairs, insurance, letting agent, empty months.
  • Location: jobs, transport links, schools and demand for rentals.
  • Interest-rate risk: rising mortgage rates can turn a profit into a loss.
  • Landlord rules and licensing in your area.

Strategy

How to position yourself for market crashes & corrections

A correction is usually defined as a fall of 10% or more from a recent high; a bear market or crash is 20% or more. They have happened many times and will happen again — the goal is to be prepared, not to predict them perfectly.

Before it happens

  • Emergency fund in cash so you never have to sell investments at the bottom.
  • Diversify across assets that don't all move together (e.g. stocks, bonds, gold, cash).
  • Hold some "dry powder" — cash ready to invest when prices are lower.
  • Avoid too much leverage and debt.
  • Have a plan written down in advance of what you'll do if markets fall 10%, 20%, 30%.

When it happens

  • Don't panic sell — historically, selling in a crash has often locked in losses.
  • Keep investing regularly — your monthly deposits buy more when prices are lower.
  • Rebalance back to your target mix.
  • Use your dry powder in stages rather than trying to catch the exact bottom.
  • Remember: some individual companies never recover. Diversified funds are more resilient than single stocks.

Taxes

Taxes

Tax can take a big bite out of your returns, and the rules change regularly and differ by country. Getting it right can save you money and keeps you on the right side of the tax authorities.

The basics to know

  • Tax-free / tax-efficient accounts (in the UK, ISAs and pensions) can shelter investments from tax up to yearly limits.
  • Capital gains tax may be due when you sell something for more than you paid.
  • Dividends, interest and rent can all be taxable income.
  • Crypto is taxable in many countries — including when you swap one coin for another.
  • Trading profits may be taxed differently depending on how and how often you trade.
  • Keep records of every buy, sell, date and price.

Allowances and rates change. Always check the latest figures with your tax authority (in the UK: gov.uk) or a qualified accountant.
